The short version

Two things went wrong, and they are roughly 60/40 by dollar. First, the trader you copied was losing money on these same picks — priced at their own entries they were down 9.1%. Second, you have no price guard, so when the market moved against a position during the seconds before your order landed, you bought it anyway at the new, worse-informed price. That second flaw carries 94% of the realized loss.

There is also a third thing that is not a cause of this loss but is the reason the loss was able to reach $150 so fast: your 111 bets were really only 28 independent coin flips, because the engine piles many bets into the same 5-minute market — up to 18 of them.

Read the statistics section before concluding the strategy is broken

At the correct unit of analysis the $150 is not statistically distinguishable from bad luck (p ≈ 0.15). The two structural flaws are real and worth fixing regardless — but one 2.9-hour session cannot tell you whether the trader can pick.

First, the arithmetic these markets run on

Every market here is a binary claim — "will ETH be up at 4:35 PM?" — that settles at exactly $1.00 if true and $0.00 if false. The price you pay per share is the market's probability estimate. Buy at 0.40 and you are paying 40¢ for a coin that pays a dollar 40% of the time.

This gives one unforgiving rule that governs everything below:

Your break-even win rate equals your average entry price. Pay 0.40 on average, and you must win more than 40% of the time. Pay 0.62, you must win more than 62%.

You paid 0.504 on average and won 40.5%. That gap — about 10 percentage points — is the entire loss. Nothing else needs explaining at the top level; the rest of this document is about why the gap opened.

Cause 2 · You buy positions the market has already written off

This is the mechanical flaw, and it is where nearly all the money went. The engine places an unbounded createMarketOrder — it accepts whatever price the order book is showing when the order arrives, however far that has drifted from the price the trader got.

Why a cheaper fill is bad news, not a bargain

This is the counter-intuitive heart of it. A market order filling below the trader's price feels like a discount. It is the opposite. In a market this fast, the price only drops because the underlying moved and the bet is now losing. The book is repricing the claim in real time, and a lower ask means the market has revised the probability downward. You are not buying the trader's bet at a discount — you are buying a materially worse bet that happens to be cheap.

Cause 3 · 111 bets were really 28 coin flips

The engine caps total exposure through MAX_ACTIVE_PCT, but nothing caps exposure per market. Each time the trader added to a position, you opened another one, so single 5-minute candles absorbed many independent bets:

You bought both sides of the same market in 14 of 28 cases

In 8 of those the two fill prices summed to more than 1.00 — worst case 1.263. Buying Up at 0.834 and Down at 0.393 means paying $1.26 for a position that pays exactly $1.00 whatever happens. The directly offsetting portion locked in about $5 of guaranteed loss, and it churned $684 of stake through the spread to no purpose. It is a small number, but it is free money handed away, and it is a sign the engine has no notion of a coherent position.

The statistics · why 111 is not 111

Taken as 111 independent bets, the result looks conclusive: z = −2.08, p ≈ 0.02. You would reject the hypothesis that this strategy breaks even.

That is the wrong unit, for two reasons. Bets cluster about four to a market, and in 14 of 28 markets every single bet shared one outcome — they were one wager wearing several hats. The independent unit is the market, not the bet.

Unit of analysisnzpVerdict
Individual bets (naive)111−2.080.02Looks like a real losing edge
Distinct markets (correct)28−1.050.15Indistinguishable from luck

So: this session does not prove the strategy loses money, and it certainly does not prove it works. 28 correlated coin flips over 2.9 hours is not enough to measure a trader's skill either way. What the session does establish, independently of sample size, is that the two structural flaws above exist in the code — those are logic errors visible in single trades, not statistical claims.

What does not fix this

The obvious instinct is "copy faster." The data rejects it. Sweeping the staleness cutoff over the actual fills makes results steadily worse, not better:

Median latency was 6.5 seconds on markets that live for 300 seconds, and the REST poller (median 8.1s) carried −$108.60 of the loss versus −$41.49 for the websocket. Latency clearly enables adverse drift — but cutting it does not fix the loss, because the guard you are missing is about price, not time. A 2-second-old trade whose price has collapsed is just as dead as a 12-second-old one. Fix the price check; treat latency as a secondary optimisation.

Next steps

In priority order. Steps 1–3 are code changes that address demonstrated logic errors. Step 4 is the one that decides whether this strategy is worth running at all — and it must be done in dry run.

  1. Add a price-deviation guard before every orderdo first

    This is the single highest-value change and it fixes a genuine missing check rather than a fitted pattern. Before placing, read the current best ask for the token and compare it to the price the trader paid. If it has drifted against you beyond a small band, skip the trade entirely.

    Backtested against this session's fills:

    skip if ask < theirs − 2c83−$30.63−4.1%
    skip if ask < theirs − 1c76−$8.85−1.3%

    A 1–3¢ band removes the loss almost entirely while still taking 76–86 of the 111 trades. Implemented in copy-trader.js, gated behind DRIFT_GUARD with the band set by MAX_ADVERSE_DRIFT (default 0.03) and MAX_OVERPAY (default 0.05):

    const drift = await driftGateCheck(trade, amount);
if (!drift.allowed) { /* journal as drift-skip, mark seen, continue */ }

    Two corrections to the obvious implementation

    Do not read asks[0]. Polymarket returns the asks array worst-price first, best last — the library's own calculateBuyMarketPrice walks it in reverse. Taking index 0 reads the most expensive level in the book and would make the guard fire more or less at random.

    Do not use top-of-book at all. The guard calls client.calculateMarketPrice(token, BUY, amount, FAK) — the exact function createMarketOrder uses to price an unpriced order. It walks real depth, so it returns the price your size will actually clear at, and the check tests the same number the order will be built with rather than an approximation of it. With a thin best ask of 0.41 backed by only $0.82 of size, a $10 order really executes at 0.60; top-of-book would have skipped a trade that was in fact only 2¢ away from the trader's price.

    Make the skip a first-class journal status (drift-skip) so the dashboard shows how often it fires — that number is your early warning that latency has degraded.

  2. Replace the unbounded market order with a bounded onedo first

    The guard above is a pre-flight check; it can still be beaten by movement in the milliseconds after you read the book. Close the hole properly at copy-trader.js:455 by sending a limit order priced at your worst acceptable fill with FAK time-in-force, instead of createMarketOrder, which by construction accepts any price.

    const limit = Math.min(1, trade.price + MAX_OVERPAY);
const order = await client.createOrder({
  tokenID: trade.asset, side: client._Side.BUY,
  price: limit, size: amountUsdc / limit,
}, { tickSize, negRisk });
await client.postOrder(order, client._OrderType.FAK);

    You keep the fill-and-kill behaviour you wanted — partial fills are fine — but you can no longer be filled at an arbitrary price. Unfilled is a perfectly good outcome; being filled at 0.39 on an 0.81 thesis is not.

  3. Cap exposure per market, and never hold both sidesthen

    Two independent rules, both cheap to add and both aimed at the concentration that let a normal losing streak reach $150:

    • One stake budget per conditionId — e.g. MAX_PER_MARKET_USDC, refusing further buys once a market's cumulative stake hits it. On this session a $30 cap would have prevented the 18-bet, $128.55 pile-up.
    • Refuse a buy on a side when you already hold the opposite side of the same conditionId. There is no version of this that is correct, and it silently pays the spread to nobody.

    Both belong beside the existing risk gate, which already walks the journal for active positions and so has the data in hand.

  4. Re-validate the trader in dry run before risking money againthe real decision

    Everything above is damage control on execution. It does not answer the question that actually determines whether this is worth running: can this wallet pick? This session says their picks lost 9.1% at their own prices, but on 28 clustered markets that reading is not significant.

    Run the engine in dry run, with the guards from steps 1–3 active, and do not go live until you can answer these:

    • Several hundred distinct markets, not several hundred bets. Track conditionId count as your real sample size — it is the only number that matters for significance.
    • Trader ROI at their own entry prices, computed exactly as in Cause 1 above. If that is not clearly positive over a large market count, no amount of execution tuning makes copying them profitable. This is the go/no-go metric.
    • Your ROI after guards, versus theirs. The gap is your true execution cost and should be small and stable.
    • Drift-skip rate. If the guard is rejecting most signals, you are structurally too slow to copy this trader at all, and that is a valid reason to stop — a strategy you cannot execute is not a strategy.

  5. Treat the profitable-looking price filters as unprovenresist

    You will notice in the tables above that simply skipping cheap entries flips this session strongly positive — filtering to fills at or above 0.50 yields +18.5% ROI and a 76% win rate. Do not deploy that. I tested roughly 25 filter variants against 28 independent clusters; a result that good appearing among them is exactly what overfitting looks like. It is also partly circular, because a high entry price in a nearly-expired market is itself downstream of the outcome.

    The honest version of that finding is already captured by step 1: cheap fills were bad because they were drift victims. Fix the cause, and do not add a price floor on top of it until several hundred markets of dry-run data say the floor earns its keep independently.

How to reproduce any figure here

Every number comes from the journal alone — win/loss from the copy.won flags the trader writes after CLOB resolution, P/L as shares − spentUsdc on wins and −spentUsdc on losses. Your realised fill price is spentUsdc / shares, which agreed with the recorded copy.price within 2¢ on all 111 rows. Sanity checks passed: both sides never won the same market, all rows settled, and the total reconciles two independent ways to −$150.09.

node -e '
const live = require("./trades-log (4).json")
  .filter(e => e.copy && e.status === "success" && e.copy.mode === "live");
const spent = live.reduce((s,e) => s + e.copy.spentUsdc, 0);
const paid  = live.reduce((s,e) => s + (e.copy.won ? e.copy.shares : 0), 0);
console.log(live.length, "bets", spent.toFixed(2), "staked", (paid-spent).toFixed(2), "net");
'

One caveat on the backtests in step 1: they filter on your realised fill price as a stand-in for the ask you would have seen a moment earlier. Those are close but not identical, so treat the recovered dollar figures as indicative rather than exact. The direction and magnitude of the effect are not in doubt; the precise cent is.
